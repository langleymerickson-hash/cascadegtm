Front office and back office, with one foundation underneath

A response to the swimlane idea, and a proposal for how to sequence the team.

The front office and back office split is the right way to divide this work. The two lanes serve different stakeholders, move at different cadences and have different definitions of done. What I would add is the layer underneath: both lanes draw from the same data foundation, and whoever builds that foundation should build it once, deliberately, before the lanes diverge.

The short version: hire one seasoned GTM engineer as a founding lead who covers both lanes, builds the shared foundation, ships the first workflows in each, and then hires the two counterparts against a proven roadmap. If there is headcount for one more, hire the back office role first.

The two lanes

Using your definition, the end product is what separates them. Front office work ends up in front of a customer. Back office work ends up in front of the business. A third set serves both audiences at once, and I have called that out separately below because it is where a two-lane model tends to leak.

The clearest early win: leveling outbound

You mentioned that some reps run outbound well and some do not. That reads as a rep performance gap, and part of it always is, but underneath it is usually a systems gap. The reps who succeed at outbound have quietly built their own machine: their own way of finding accounts, their own research routine, their own sense of when to reach out. That machine is private, unmanaged and walks out the door when they do. The reps who struggle are doing the same job without it.

This is the problem GTM engineering is best at solving, because almost every input to good outbound is a data and tooling problem before it is a talent problem:

  • Reach. A single data provider finds valid contact data for roughly two thirds of a list. Waterfall enrichment across providers lifts that into the high eighties or better, so the accounts a rep cannot reach stop being a function of which tool they happen to use.
  • Timing. Signals like funding, hiring, leadership changes and product usage decide whether a message lands. Watching for them by hand favors whoever is most diligent that week. Watching for them systematically gives every rep the same trigger at the same moment.
  • Research and personalization. The twenty minutes a good rep spends preparing an account is the real constraint on their volume. An agent doing that preparation, with the rep reviewing and owning the send, raises the floor without flattening the voice.
  • Suppression and routing. Shared rules stop two reps working the same buyer and keep outbound out of live deals, which is the failure mode that makes leaders distrust outbound systems in the first place.

The goal is to make the best rep's process the team's floor. The honest version of that claim: tooling does not close the whole gap. Discovery, judgment and relationship building still separate great reps from good ones, and they should. What should not separate them is who has better data, better timing and more hours to research. That part is buildable, and it is the first thing I would want to build in the front office lane.

The measure I would watch is the spread rather than the average. If the system is working, the variance between top-quartile and median reps on outbound-sourced pipeline compresses, because the parts of the job that were private advantage are now infrastructure.

The workflows that sit in the middle

Not every workflow lands cleanly in one lane. Contracting is the clearest example: the audience is the customer and, at the same moment, your general counsel, finance and deal desk. The document goes out, but the approvals, the risk posture and the revenue implications all live inside. There are several like it.

WorkflowExternal audienceInternal audience
Contracting and redliningThe prospect or customerGeneral counsel, finance and deal desk
Quoting, pricing and approvalsThe buyer evaluating the offerDeal desk and finance own the guardrails

EscalationsThe customer waiting on an answerSupport, product and leadership

These matter more than their count suggests, for two reasons. They sit close to revenue recognition, so they are high value and low tolerance for error. And they are the workflows most likely to fall through the seam if two lanes are staffed with no shared owner, because each engineer can reasonably argue they belong to the other lane. I would want them explicitly assigned rather than assumed, and in the early phase I would hold them myself, since they are also the workflows that most benefit from someone who can see both audiences at once.

Why the foundation needs one owner

If both lanes are staffed at once with no shared owner, the predictable outcome is two data models, two vendor evaluations, two sets of SOPs and two definitions of an account. That is not a hypothetical failure mode, it is the most common one I see in growth-stage GTM orgs, and it is expensive to unwind later because every workflow built on top inherits the divergence.

A founding lead solves this structurally, in four ways:

  • Build the foundation once. Identity, enrichment, freshness and ownership are shared inputs. Sequencing them first makes every workflow in both lanes faster to build and cheaper to maintain.
  • Sequence across both lanes. A leader who sees the full roadmap can spot where a back office data fix unblocks three front office workflows, and order the work accordingly. Two separate engineers optimize their own lane, which is rational and slower overall.
  • Operate at peer level. Much of this work is negotiation with Product, Business Systems, RevOps, Data and Finance about priorities, access and ownership. That conversation goes differently when it happens leader to leader.
  • Own the seam. The workflows that serve both audiences at once, described above, belong to neither lane by default. They need one person accountable for them, or they quietly become the work nobody picks up.
  • Hire better counterparts. Someone who has done the work defines the roles, sets the bar, runs the loop and ramps the hires. Those are much better hires than a roadmap written before anyone has built anything.

The sequence

One lead first, then both lanes staffed as the work proves itself. This is deliberately not a proposal to hire a manager. Phase 1 is roughly eighty percent hands-on building.

Phase 1Months 0 to 6

One founding lead, both lanes

Build the shared foundation once, then ship the first workflows in each lane. Roughly 80 percent hands-on building. The output is a working roadmap, a data foundation both lanes stand on, proof in production, and a defined hiring bar for the two counterpart roles.

  • Agent-readiness audit and problem ranking
  • Data architecture, availability and quality pipeline
  • Vendor selection and SOPs
  • First front office and first back office workflow live
Phase 2Months 6 to 12

Lead plus back office engineer

The first counterpart hire takes the internal-facing lane. The lead keeps building in the front office, where the revenue impact is most direct, and starts splitting time toward architecture, QA and cross-functional problem shaping.

  • Back office GTM engineer owns forecasting, hygiene and reporting workflows
  • Lead continues front office builds
  • Maintenance and observability formalized
  • ROI experiment design with a holdout
Phase 3Months 12 to 18

Lead plus both lanes staffed

The front office counterpart joins. The lead shifts primarily to shaping problems, working across Product, Business Systems, RevOps and Finance, extending the data architecture ahead of the roadmap, and owning QA as the surface area grows.

  • Two dedicated engineers, one per lane
  • Lead owns architecture, QA and cross-org sequencing
  • Agent registry and cost model in quarterly planning
  • Team scales against a measured roadmap

What forces the second and third hire

Agentic workflows are not build-once assets. They need monitoring, prompt tuning, data drift checks and updates as the GTM strategy changes. In my experience each live workflow needs somewhere between three and six hours a month of care. Early on that is about twenty percent of one engineer's time, which is comfortable. The curve is what matters.

Workflows liveMaintenance per monthShare of one engineerWhat it means
5 workflows live15 to 30 hrs10 to 20 percentOne engineer builds and maintains comfortably.
12 workflows live36 to 72 hrs25 to 45 percentBuild velocity drops. QA is the first thing to slip.
20 workflows live60 to 120 hrs40 to 75 percentOne engineer is mostly maintaining. The second hire is overdue.
30 or more90 to 180 hrs60 percent to over one FTETwo engineers maintain and build. The lead holds architecture and QA.

Assumes roughly 160 working hours a month per engineer. These are planning estimates rather than precise figures, but the shape holds: maintenance grows with every workflow shipped, and it is the reason a single engineer cannot own both lanes indefinitely.

Cost, ROI and the Finance partnership

GTM engineering spends real money in three places: vendor platforms, model tokens and engineering hours. At most companies all three stay invisible until someone in Finance asks, and by then the answer is reconstructed from expense reports rather than modeled. I would want this role to own making those economics legible to your controller and CFO, before the spend rather than after it.

That means two different conversations. The short-term one is unit economics: what a specific workflow costs to run at our volumes, and whether to build it or buy it. The long-term one is the opex trajectory and the return: where this lands in three years as workflows accumulate, vendors consolidate and model prices keep falling, and what the P&L actually got for the investment.

The question Finance asksHorizonWhat this role provides
What will this workflow cost to run?Short termA per-workflow model before the build: model tokens in and out, platform and vendor fees, and the engineering hours to run it, at Lattice's actual volumes.
What happens at three times the volume?Short termSensitivity on the real drivers: number of users, run frequency, model tier, caching and batching. Volume changes the answer more than price negotiation does.
Do we build this or buy it?Short termA recommendation with total cost of ownership on both sides, including the maintenance hours a build carries and the switching cost a buy avoids.
What is the opex trajectory?Long termA rolling view that accounts for the maintenance curve above, vendor consolidation as workflows merge, and falling model prices working in our favor.
What did we get for it?Long termROI measured against a holdout rather than inferred from point-in-time data, so the number survives scrutiny in a board deck or a diligence process.
What are we spending in total?Long termOne consolidated view of GTM engineering spend across vendors, tokens and hours, owned by one person instead of reconstructed from expense reports.

The cadence matters as much as the model. Cost and ROI for GTM engineering belong in quarterly and annual planning next to headcount, not in a one-off analysis when someone questions an invoice. That is also what makes the next hire an easy conversation instead of a debate.

This is not theoretical for me. I built and published a cost model for exactly this problem: pick a use case, set the team size, usage and model tier, and it returns the monthly and annual cost with every assumption visible, exportable to a spreadsheet with live formulas so a controller can change an assumption and watch the model recalculate. I would bring that here and tune it to Lattice's actual stack and volumes.

Role: Founding GTM Engineering Lead

Mission. Build the systems and the team that make Lattice's revenue org measurably faster, more accurate and more visible with AI, across both client-facing and internal workflows, without letting the two diverge.

Reports to the CRO. Partners with RevOps, Business Systems, Data, Finance, Product and Enablement. Scope begins as an individual contributor with a mandate, and grows into a team of two to four.

First-year outcomes

First 30 days

Learn the business and audit the foundation

Meet leaders and individual contributors across Sales, Marketing, CS, RevOps, Finance and Data. Shadow the actual workflows. Run an agent-readiness audit on data architecture, availability, accuracy and ownership, and confirm which core GTM motions have documented SOPs.

Days 30 to 60

Rank the problems and build the roadmap

Rank problems by revenue impact, confidence in the solution and time to ship. Listen to call recordings. Audit the GTM systems directly. Bring the roadmap and the prioritization method to stakeholders, then present it cross-functionally so the revenue org owns it.

Days 60 to 90

Ship in both lanes and set the bar

Resolve the foundation blockers first, then ship one front office and one back office workflow with SOPs, evaluations and a QA gate in place. Define the two counterpart roles, the hiring bar and the interview loop.

Months 4 to 12

Scale the system and the team

Six to ten workflows live across both lanes with owners, documentation and measured results. Hire and ramp the back office counterpart. Extend the data architecture ahead of the roadmap.

Ongoing

Make the economics legible to Finance

A cost model the controller and CFO can open, follow and challenge, covering both what a workflow costs to run today and what it costs at three times the volume. GTM engineering cost and ROI reviewed as part of quarterly and annual planning rather than reconstructed after the fact.

What this role owns

  • The foundation. Data architecture, availability, accuracy and ownership for GTM workflows. The canonical account and contact model, the enrichment pipeline and the freshness cadence.
  • The roadmap. Problems ranked by revenue impact, confidence and effort, agreed with stakeholders and reviewed on a cadence.
  • The workflows. Hands-on builds in both lanes in Phase 1, then in the front office as counterparts come on.
  • Vendor selection, cost and ROI. One evaluation and one spend line per capability, a per-workflow cost model the controller and CFO can gut-check, and the ROI case that justifies the next build.
  • SOPs and governance. Documented motions, human-in-the-loop points, brand guardrails, an agent registry and scoped credentials.
  • Quality. Evaluations before rollout, monitoring after, and a defined bar for what is allowed to touch a customer.
  • The team. Role definitions, hiring bar, interview loop, ramp and ongoing management as the function scales.

What this role does not own

The CRM as a system of record stays with RevOps. Integration security and platform administration stay with Business Systems. The warehouse and enterprise BI stay with Data. This role builds the GTM workflow layer on top of those systems and is a partner to each, which keeps the boundaries clean and avoids duplicating teams that already exist.

What we are looking for

  • Eight or more years in GTM Ops, RevOps or GTM systems, with recent hands-on building rather than only oversight.
  • Deep experience with modern GTM tooling: Salesforce, Gong, HubSpot, Apollo & tools like Clay, Clari, Outreach, and more, with working fluency in SQL, Python and APIs.
  • Comfortable personally building and deploying LLM powered tooling and AI agent workflows from zero to one, including vendor evaluations, and human-in-the-loop design.
  • Has deployed both front office (client-facing) and back office (internal-facing) GTM workflows and understands the data foundations and agent readiness layers behind each.
  • Strong judgment on where AI creates leverage and when humans should shine, with the discipline and foresight to build only where there is an advantage.
  • Has experience hiring and managing technical teams: business systems engineers, GTM engineers, or other technical roles.
  • Operates at peer level with functional leaders in Business Systems, RevOps, Product, and Finance.
  • Deep experience building, managing, and executing against a cross-functional roadmap.
  • Comfortable in the numbers. Owner of GTM engineering cost models for the controller and CFO and can defend the model's assumptions line by line.
  • High ownership in ambiguity. This role starts with a blank roadmap that is yours to develop.

The two counterpart roles

Both are individual contributor roles reporting to the lead. Defining them now, and hiring them against a roadmap that already has production proof behind it, is what makes them successful hires rather than expensive experiments.

Client-facing workflows

GTM Engineer, Front Office

Owns the workflows whose end product reaches a customer: prospecting research and personalization, inbound response, call preparation and follow-up, battlecards and lifecycle personalization. Partners closely with Sales and Marketing leadership, and is measured on pipeline, conversion and reply quality.

Hire second if the lead focuses here first, or first if front office is the more urgent gap.
Internal-facing workflows

GTM Engineer, Back Office

Owns the workflows whose end product serves the business: forecasting accuracy, pipeline hygiene, executive reporting, territory and capacity ops, attribution and deal desk support. Partners closely with RevOps, Finance and Data, and is measured on accuracy, cycle time and trust in the numbers.

The first counterpart hire in the sequence below, which frees the lead to stay in the front office.

If there is headcount for one hire alongside the lead, I would hire the back office engineer first. The front office work carries the most direct revenue impact, so that is where a founding lead should keep their hands the longest.

Why I am proposing this

I designed this around what I think gives Lattice the best outcome: one foundation, built once, with both lanes sequenced against it, and a team that grows only as fast as the proof does. I have built in both lanes, I have led GTM Ops functions, and I would be glad to hold both while we prove the model and hire the counterparts. If your thinking has gone in a different direction, I am genuinely interested in where, and this is a starting point rather than a finished answer.
