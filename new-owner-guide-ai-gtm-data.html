Field note · A guide for the new business-systems owner

You Just Took the Job. Now Someone Wants to Connect Claude to the CRM.

A field guide for the incoming IT and business-systems leader who owns security and integrity alone, wants to back an ambitious AI roadmap, and has real, grounded doubts about handing company data to Anthropic or OpenAI. What the agreements actually bind, which fields to hide on which objects, and the first move that still looks smart in two years.

By Langley Erickson · CascadeGTM · July 2026

A few weeks ago I talked with someone who had just made a deliberate jump. He left a company that was slow on AI for one with a real appetite for it, because he did not want to sit out the biggest platform shift of his career. It was a good, brave call. Then he described his first month, and the shape of his problem came into focus.

He now owns all of it — business systems, security, system integrity — by himself. No dedicated security team. No prior tour of duty running a stack with AI agents wired into it. And on his desk, on day thirty, sits a roadmap the company is genuinely excited about and two newly hired GTM engineers who want to start connecting Claude and ChatGPT into the CRM, the data warehouse, the call recordings and the contract repository. He wants to say yes. He also knows that the integration decisions he makes this quarter are the ones a future audit, a future breach post-mortem, or a future acquirer will judge him on. He is making calls today that are hard to unmake later, with less support than the job really needs.

His specific worry was sharper than the usual hand-waving about "AI and data." He knows the paid tiers don't train on your data. He has read the enterprise terms. His unease is that the terms feel paper-thin — a promise, not a wall. His logic, which I think is completely fair: OpenAI started as an open-source nonprofit and its posture changed. Tech companies have quietly rewritten data terms before. Why should he bet his company's most sensitive data on a policy page that could read differently in eighteen months?

This guide is the answer I gave him, expanded and sourced. It is written for him, but it fits anyone who owns systems and security at a company moving fast on AI without a big team behind them. The short version: your instinct to distrust a bare promise is correct, and the fix is not to withhold everything — it is to make the promise enforceable, hand the model less, and sequence your first move so it ages well.

First, separate the fear you can retire from the one you should keep

Two different worries usually travel together under "I'm nervous about AI and our data," and merging them is what leads good leaders to freeze. Pull them apart.

The fear you can mostly retire is "they'll train on our data." On the paid tiers this is genuinely settled. Anthropic's commercial terms state that Claude for Work, Enterprise, Education and API data is not used for model training, and commercial customers were explicitly carved out of the September 2025 consumer policy change. OpenAI says the same for its enterprise and API products. This part is real and you can stop losing sleep over it in isolation.

The fear you should keep is exactly the one he named: a policy is not a contract, and a contract is only as good as its teeth. He is right to notice that Anthropic quietly changed its consumer training-and-retention posture in August 2025 and gave users a late-September opt-out deadline — the company that built its brand on privacy still moved the line for consumers. His extrapolation is reasonable: if the consumer line moved once, why trust the commercial line to hold forever? The answer isn't "trust them more." It's "stop relying on trust and start relying on the instruments that make a change like that a breach of contract instead of a product update." That distinction is the whole game, and it's the next section.

Is the agreement really paper-thin? Turning a promise into a wall

Here's the honest reframe. A privacy policy is a promise the vendor can revise. A negotiated enterprise agreement with the right addenda is a contract the vendor breaches if they revise it against you. And in the U.S. there's a regulator that has repeatedly said changing the data rules after the fact is itself illegal.

Start with the regulator, because it directly addresses his "they'll just change the terms" fear. The FTC has stated plainly and more than once that it may be unfair or deceptive for a company to quietly rewrite its privacy policy or terms of service to give itself new freedom to use customer data — including for AI training — without clear notice and affirmative consent. Its position is that a business that collected data under one set of commitments cannot unilaterally renege on those commitments after the fact, and it has repeatedly acted on it — against a genetic-testing company that retroactively broadened data sharing, and against Avast for selling "anonymized" browsing data that still carried identifiers. The FTC has since reiterated the point specifically for AI companies: uphold your privacy and confidentiality commitments or face enforcement. So the nightmare scenario — "they'll flip a switch and start using our data" — isn't a frictionless product update. For a covered vendor it's a contract breach and a federal-enforcement exposure at the same time. That doesn't make it impossible. It makes it expensive and slow enough that you get warning and recourse, which is exactly what a promise-versus-wall distinction is supposed to buy you.

Then thicken the contract yourself. The instruments that convert the thin promise into the thick wall already exist, and getting them is your leverage as the buyer:

  • A negotiated enterprise agreement and DPA, not click-through terms. The commercial no-training commitment lives here as a contractual term, not a blog post.
  • A Zero Data Retention (ZDR) arrangement for your sensitive workflows. Under ZDR, Anthropic does not store inputs or outputs except as needed to comply with law or prevent misuse. It shrinks the retention window from the default (API inputs and outputs auto-deleted within 30 days) toward not-stored-at-all. Note the honest caveat: even under ZDR, safety-classifier results are still retained and law/misuse exceptions still apply — ZDR is a very short leash, not a magic eraser.
  • A SOC 2 Type II report and, if you touch regulated data, a BAA. Anthropic holds SOC 2 Type II covering the Claude API, with audit reports available to enterprise customers under NDA through its trust center, and signs HIPAA BAAs for enterprise and direct-API customers (not for Free, Pro, Max or Team). These are independent attestations and legal instruments, not marketing.
  • The buyer's real leverage: you can walk. A vendor that materially changes terms against a negotiated enterprise contract hands you an exit and a claim. That's the wall.
The reframe to take to your CEO

You are not deciding whether to "trust Anthropic or OpenAI." You are deciding whether to rely on a revisable promise or a negotiated contract backed by SOC 2 attestation, a ZDR retention floor, and a federal regulator that treats a quiet reversal as an enforceable offense. The first is paper-thin. The second is the thickest wall available short of never using the tool — and it is thick enough to build on.

One more thing worth saying directly, because he asked it: could a vendor still betray a signed enterprise agreement someday? In principle, yes — no contract makes betrayal physically impossible. But the same is true of your payroll processor, your cloud provider, and the CRM already holding all this data. You already trust vendors with sensitive data every day on exactly these instruments. The right posture isn't to treat AI vendors as uniquely untrustworthy; it's to hold them to the same enforceable standard you already hold everyone else to, and to hand them less in the first place. Which brings us to the part you control completely.

The risk you own outright: what the agent can actually see

Here's the liberating part for someone in his seat. The vendor agreement is a shared risk you negotiate. But the biggest lever is entirely yours and requires no one's signature: what data the agent is even allowed to touch. A model can't leak, retain, or mishandle a field it was never shown. Most of the exposure in a GTM stack comes not from the vendor's posture but from connecting an agent to far more than its job requires.

And the threat here is concrete, not theoretical. In 2025 a researcher showed that a Salesforce Agentforce agent could be made to email sensitive CRM records to an outside address by hiding instructions inside a public web-to-lead form — no authentication, scored 9.4 out of 10, dubbed ForcedLeak. The lesson for a new owner isn't "Salesforce is unsafe." It's that an agent which can read outside input, holds broad CRM access, and can send messages is a loaded weapon — and the fix is to narrow each of those three the moment you wire anything up.

Exposure isn't uniform across the funnel, so your controls shouldn't be either. Risk climbs with three properties: how legally binding the data is, how individually identifiable it is, and how company-material a single number is.

Which fields to hide, on which objects

This is the part he most wanted and the part most write-ups skip. "Scope the connector" is easy to say. Here is what it actually means at the object-and-field level, using the Salesforce object model as the reference (HubSpot and Dynamics have direct equivalents). The governing question for every field, borrowed from a Salesforce admin I trust: if you wouldn't email this field in plain text to an outside vendor, look hard at why an agent is putting it in a prompt.

SEND = low-risk, connect with a scoped view  ·  MASK = tokenize/redact before the prompt leaves your perimeter  ·  BLOCK = keep out of any autonomous agent context

A few implementation notes that will save him an audit finding:

  • Field-level security is the enforcement layer, and agents inherit it. Agentforce and similar tools operate within the user's permission model — they respect field-level security and cannot access what the running user cannot access. So the move is to run agents as a dedicated, least-privilege integration user with a permission set that exposes only the "safe" columns above — never a rep's full profile, never a system admin.
  • Masking inherits your misconfigurations. A trust layer that masks PII is only as good as the field classification under it: a Social Security field a user can read will flow into prompts unless you classify and mask it explicitly. Do the field-classification audit before you turn anything on, because the default mask set covers common PII but not industry-specific identifiers.
  • Schema is a leak path people forget. With Anthropic's BAA, PHI must appear only in message content — never in JSON schema field names, enum values, or regex patterns, because compiled schemas are cached separately and don't get the same protection. Don't name a field patient_ssn in your tool definition.
  • Masking protects the boundary, not the browser. Once a masked value is de-masked for display, that summary is now in the UI, in screenshots, in clipboard buffers. Governance doesn't end at the model call.

The single most durable control on this whole list is boring: route every model call through one gateway that enforces the scoping, masking and logging centrally, so it doesn't depend on each GTM engineer remembering to do it right in every connector. It's also the fix for the most common failure pattern in the field — a signed ZDR or BAA that a developer silently bypassed by making one API call without the per-call ZDR flag set. A gateway makes the safe path the only path.

The option that sidesteps the whole debate: bring the model to the data

For his most sensitive workloads there's a third posture beyond "send it" and "withhold it," and it's the one I'd most want him to know exists on day thirty, because it quietly retires the paper-thin-agreement worry for the data that scares him most. Instead of sending data out to the model, run the model where the data already lives.

This is shipping today. Snowflake's Cortex runs LLM inference and PII redaction directly in SQL, with no external API calls and no data leaving Snowflake. Salesforce's Data Cloud Zero Copy lets agents act on data that stays in your Snowflake or Databricks lake, and its Trust Layer can run Anthropic models inside the Salesforce trust boundary on Bedrock so traffic never leaves the VPC. For a leader worried about a vendor's future posture, this is the cleanest answer: for health scoring, usage analysis and forecasting, keep inference inside your boundary and let only derived results cross the line. It doesn't replace Claude or ChatGPT for the many use cases where sending scoped, masked data out is fine — it gives him a second gear for exactly the data he's least willing to expose.

His actual question: what's the first move?

He didn't want a philosophy. He wanted to know what to do on Monday that supports the roadmap, buys him the experience he jumped for, and doesn't blow up later. Here is the ninety-day version I'd hand him — deliberately lightweight, because a twelve-month governance program that ships nothing until month twelve is its own kind of failure, and because the durable move is inventory-and-classification first, not policy-binder first.

1

Days 1–15 · Inventory and amnesty, before you build anything

You can't govern what you can't see, and your biggest live exposure today is almost certainly shadow AI — reps already pasting deals into personal ChatGPT. Announce that nobody's tool gets killed for existing, then inventory what's actually running: API keys and OAuth grants in your identity provider, LLM domains in expense reports, any MCP connections the GTM engineers already stood up. Expect to find more than the survey admits.

2

Days 10–30 · Classify the data before it touches a model

Run the field-level audit from the table above across every object an agent might read. Tag each field send / mask / block. This one artifact is the thing your future auditor, acquirer, or security reviewer will ask for — and the thing that lets you say yes to the GTM engineers with specifics instead of a nervous maybe.

3

Days 20–40 · Get the paper right, once

Negotiate the enterprise agreement + DPA, turn on ZDR for anything in the mask/block tiers, pull the SOC 2 Type II report from the trust center, and sign a BAA if you touch regulated data. This is the step that converts the "paper-thin promise" into an enforceable wall — do it before production, not after.

4

Days 30–60 · Stand up one gateway, then pick one low-risk pilot

Route all model access through a single gateway that enforces scoping, masking and logging centrally. Then greenlight the safest high-value use case first — prospect research or account enrichment, which lives in the green tier and lets your GTM engineers ship a real win while you watch the logs. This is how you get the experience you took the job for, on the use case least likely to hurt you.

5

Days 45–75 · Prove the controls on a messy case

Deliberately run one harder use case — something touching customer usage data — through the full path: scoped connector, masking, gateway logging, human-in-the-loop on any external send. The gaps you find in one real dry run are worth more than any forty-page policy. Fix them before you scale.

6

Days 60–90 · Write the one-page rules and a quarterly review

Turn what you learned into a short standard: which tiers of data may leave the perimeter, what must run in-tenant, the least-privilege agent-user pattern, mandatory gateway routing, and human approval before any autonomous external action. Put contracting and the connector inventory on a quarterly cadence. Ship the page; don't gold-plate it.

Notice what this sequence does for his specific situation. It never blocks the roadmap — the GTM engineers are shipping a real pilot inside sixty days. It front-loads the two artifacts (the data classification and the contract) that make his decisions defensible later. And it starts him on the easy use cases, so the experience he builds is real without being reckless. He gets to be the person who said yes and got it right, which is the entire reason he took the job.

The leaders who regret their AI-data call in two years aren't the ones who connected Claude or ChatGPT with real guardrails. They're the ones who connected everything with none — or who connected nothing while the company that needed them to move stalled out.

Caution has a cost too — and it lands on your name

One honest thing to leave him with, because it's the trap I see careful new owners fall into. Saying no to everything feels like the safe, responsible choice. It isn't a neutral act. He jumped to this company precisely to be near the frontier; a posture of blanket refusal would slowly rebuild the exact situation he left, except now it's his signature on it.

Leadership takeaways

The version for each seat around him, so he can bring the right people in at the right moment.

The new business-systems owner (you)

Your instinct to distrust a bare promise is correct — act on it by thickening the contract, not by withholding data. Do the field classification and the enterprise agreement before production. Route everything through one gateway. Start on the green-tier use cases. You will have shipped a real pilot and built the defensible paper trail inside a quarter, alone, without a security team.

CEO / Founder who hired you

The person you hired needs two things from you: air cover to negotiate real enterprise agreements (not click-through terms) and a mandate that new AI connectors get registered before they get credentials. Back the ninety-day sequence publicly so the GTM engineers see governance as the fast path, not the roadblock.

GTM engineers building the agents

Field-level scoping and gateway routing aren't friction added to your build — they're the reason your build survives the first security review. Run agents as a least-privilege integration user, send banded economics instead of raw amounts, and never wire an autonomous agent to the live contract repo. You'll ship faster because nothing gets clawed back.

CFO / Finance

This is a portfolio decision, not a binary. The control posture is a $25–60k line, not a security-department budget, and it's what turns "we use AI on customer data" into a documented, defensible position for the next raise or audit. The cost of blanket caution is a slower pipeline and a weaker forecast — a real number on the other side of the ledger.

Legal / outside counsel

The enforceable instruments are the DPA, the ZDR addendum, the BAA where regulated data is involved, and the FTC's stated position that a retroactive, unconsented data-use change is itself unfair or deceptive. That combination is what makes an AI vendor's commitment a wall rather than a promise. Prioritize getting them signed before the first production workload, and keep an exit clause tied to material data-term changes.
