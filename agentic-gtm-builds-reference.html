Three Agentic GTM Builds, End to End

A working reference for designing agentic solutions to real GTM problems. Three worked examples, each with the before and after, the reference architecture, the end-user workflow, the operating procedures and the launch playbook. The point across all three: the right architecture depends on the shape of the work, and a good build buys the commodity and builds only where there is an advantage.

By Langley Erickson · CascadeGTM · July 2026

Each example collapses to its name so this doc is easy to scan and easy to walk through in a conversation. Open the one you want. All three assume a Series C company moving from Phase 3 to Phase 4 of the agent adoption curve, with the heads of Revenue Operations, Business Systems and GTM Engineering collaborating. For the full operating model behind these builds, the swimlanes and the collaboration RACI, see the companion field note.

The three builds at a glance

Three common GTM problems, three deliberately different answers. Read the shape of each before opening it: one builds a thin agent layer on an all-in-one platform, one needs a custom autonomous stack, and one builds a thin layer on tools you already own. The tell is how the agents run. Two of the three are batch, sequential and human-reviewed, so they use a simple scheduler; only the event-driven, branching, autonomous build needs a stateful orchestrator like LangGraph and per-run observability. Each example says why in its own section.

Why Apollo plus Pocus, and the honest tradeoff versus Clay. For a sales-led growth company, consolidation is the win: Apollo is a single vendor for the 275M-contact database, native enrichment and the sequence engine, and Pocus adds the signal-based prioritization that turns a big list into a ranked one. One bill, one interface, fast to stand up, and reps live in one tool. That is the right default when speed and simplicity matter more than squeezing the last point of data coverage.
What you give up by not using Clay, and where it costs you. This is a real trade, not a free lunch. Data coverage: Apollo is single-source, so on thin ICPs, EU contacts or niche technical personas its coverage trails a Clay waterfall across many providers, where teams report enrichment moving from roughly 40 percent to the high 80s. If your ICP is US SaaS, the gap is small; if it is not, it is real pipeline you never reach. Workflow flexibility: Apollo works the way Apollo decided, so complex multi-source logic or bespoke enrichment cascades are harder than in Clay's open table. Cost shape: Apollo is predictable per-seat pricing; Clay is credit-based and can be cheaper or far more expensive depending on how many providers each waterfall hits. ROI read: for this motion the ROI comes from signal-based prioritization plus the cited angle, not from maximal enrichment, so Apollo plus Pocus captures most of the upside at lower operating complexity. The honest escalation trigger: if data coverage on hard ICPs becomes the constraint on pipeline, add Clay as an enrichment layer feeding Apollo, which is exactly how many mature teams run both.

Eval + drift monitoring (Langfuse)First, the division of labor, because it drives cost. Managed Agents already gives you observability for free: it captures the traces, tool calls, errors, latency and cost of every run in its dashboard. What it does not give you is evaluation, a judgment of whether the output was good, and that is the layer you add here. Monitoring is the LLM-as-a-judge pattern: a separate, more capable judge model scores a sample of drafted angles each day against a rubric (is the why-now accurate, is the citation real, is it on-brand, does it hallucinate a claim), writing a pass or fail score. Offline, the same rubric runs on a labeled golden set before any prompt change and blocks the deploy if quality drops. Online, the sampled scores drive a drift alert. It is not a human reading every message; it is an automated judge on a sample, with a person recalibrating the judge periodically. Langfuse is the named default here because it gives RevOps a dashboard for quality trends, prompt versioning and drift charts without building them. If there is pressure to cut the stack, the leaner option is to skip Langfuse and run the judge as a small step inside the daily job that scores a sample and logs the result, since Managed Agents already provides the traces. Either way you write the rubric yourself; the only question is whether you buy the eval harness or build a light one.GTM Engineering
When suppression does need an agent: messy account data. The Salesforce Flow above assumes clean data. Many companies do not have it: duplicate accounts where the master is flagged a customer but the duplicate looks like a fresh prospect, contacts associated with both the duplicates and the master, and type or churn flags that are not reliably maintained. In that world a single-record lookup gives the wrong answer, and the lead you are about to email cannot be cleanly traced to the record that would tell you it is already a customer. Deciding "is this really a prospect, or a disguised customer, churned account or open deal" now means weighing several fields that disagree with each other, which is a judgment under uncertainty, and that is where an agent genuinely earns its place.

The design: a resolution agent reads across the candidate account, its likely duplicates, the associated contacts, open opportunities and type and churn fields, and returns a classification (prospect, customer, churned, open opportunity) with a confidence score. Routing is by confidence: high-confidence suppress is auto-suppressed, high-confidence eligible passes through, and anything the agent is unsure about goes to a human review queue rather than being silently guessed. The human decisions become labeled data to tune the agent over time. This runs as a Managed Agent step in the same daily job, and its outputs feed the same eval monitoring as the angle agent. RevOps owns the classification logic; GTM Engineering builds and tunes the agent; the human-review queue is a rep or ops task RevOps owns.

Be honest that this is a symptom, not a cure. A resolution agent makes outbound safe despite a broken account model, which is real value now, but the root problem is data quality. Business Systems should be fixing the duplicates and the contact-to-account associations upstream in parallel, so the agent is a bridge, not permanent cover for a CRM that needs cleaning. The best version runs the agent to unblock outbound today and shrinks its job every quarter as the underlying data gets fixed.
Why no LangGraph or agent-observability platform for the core workflow. The main pipeline runs in batch (a daily job), the steps run in a fixed line (rank, enrich, write angle, draft), and a rep approves every send. That combination does not need a stateful orchestrator: a simple scheduler runs the sequence, and the human is the last-mile quality gate. What it does still need is eval and drift monitoring on the agents that write copy or classify accounts, so quality does not decay silently. Contrast this with Example 2, where the agents are event-driven, branch per lead, and act autonomously, which is what actually justifies LangGraph and per-run observability.

The standard operating procedures that make it safe

These are the operating rules that keep a signal-led outbound motion safe, on-brand and economical.

  • Approval before send: every first touch is a rep's decision. The agent drafts and the sequence loads, but a human signs off, which keeps deliverability and brand safe.
  • Signal before volume: reps work the Pocus-ranked accounts, not a raw export. Prioritization is the point; blasting the whole database is what got outbound throttled in the first place.
  • Suppression is enforced, not trusted: a Salesforce Flow keeps an outbound-eligible flag current, so customers, recently churned accounts, open opportunities and accounts already in an active sequence are excluded before any draft. Outbound never touches a warm or conflicted account.
  • One angle, cited: the why-now hook must link to the signal or source that justifies it, so a rep can trust it and a prospect gets a real reason, not a hallucinated one.
  • Send and credit discipline: daily send caps protect domain reputation, and because Apollo cost splits into predictable per-seat platform fees plus variable enrichment credits, a credit ceiling with alerts keeps the variable half from surprising Finance.
  • Scoped keys and audit: Apollo and Salesforce connect with least-privilege service credentials owned by Business Systems, and every enrichment and send is logged.

The launch playbook: who leads, who supports

Standing this up is mostly configuration plus one thin build. Each stage names a lead and the supporting roles.

1

Shape the problem and the motion

RevOps leads, GTME and BizSys consult

A sales-led growth company is generating pipeline by having reps hand-build lists and write cold outreach from scratch, and the results are inconsistent: the strong reps have a private prospecting machine, the rest do not. RevOps frames the value, which is lifting the whole team to the best rep's floor and working accounts by signal rather than by list. GTME confirms what Apollo and Pocus already do so the build only adds the missing angle layer. Business Systems scopes the Apollo and Salesforce access and the send guardrails.

RevOps owns the motion and the goal. GTME maps buy-versus-build. BizSys scopes access and guardrails.
2

Configure the platform, build the thin layer

GTME builds, BizSys provisions, RevOps defines done

The heavy lifting is configuration, not code: Business Systems connects Apollo and Salesforce with scoped credentials and sets the bidirectional sync, and RevOps sets the Pocus signal model, the ICP and the territory rules. RevOps also defines the suppression logic (skip customers, recently churned accounts, open opportunities and accounts already in an active sequence). How GTM Engineering implements it forks on data quality: a Salesforce Flow flagging eligible accounts if the data is clean, or a resolution agent with a human-review queue if duplicates and weak contact associations make a lookup unreliable. GTME then builds the one piece Apollo lacks, the research-and-angle agent that writes a cited why-now hook. RevOps defines what a good angle looks like and what reps must never send.

BizSys owns the platform connection and any upstream dedupe. RevOps owns the signal model, ICP and suppression logic. GTME builds the angle agent and the suppression Flow or resolution agent.
3

QA the signals, the angles and the suppression

All three sign before it reaches a prospect

GTME validates the angle agent against a set of real accounts: is the why-now accurate, is the citation real, does it ever hallucinate a claim. If a resolution agent is in play, GTME also checks its classification accuracy and that low-confidence accounts route to the review queue rather than getting auto-suppressed or auto-sent. RevOps confirms the suppression rules match reality and that the Pocus ranking matches how they would prioritize. Business Systems confirms the Flow or agent fires reliably, the credentials are least-privilege, the send caps hold and the sync does not create duplicates. Three checks, one gate, before a single sequence sends.

GTME owns angle-agent QA. RevOps owns suppression-rule and prioritization QA. BizSys owns Flow, access and deliverability QA.
4

Train reps and roll out with a holdout

RevOps owns adoption

RevOps trains reps on the ranked queue: what the signals mean, how to edit and approve, when to override the agent. A holdout of reps runs the old hand-built motion for a quarter so the lift in meetings and pipeline is proven, not assumed. GTME documents the angle logic and the edge cases.

RevOps owns training and the holdout. GTME documents the logic.
5

Operate, measure and tune

Shared ownership, clear lanes

GTME tunes the angle agent and watches enrichment and send costs. Business Systems keeps the Apollo and Salesforce sync healthy and rotates credentials. RevOps watches the metric that justified it: qualified meetings and pipeline created per rep, and the spread between top and median reps compressing as the private prospecting machine becomes shared infrastructure.

GTME maintains the agent and cost. BizSys maintains the sync. RevOps owns the pipeline outcome.

Why this one needs LangGraph and LangSmith, and the others do not. Three properties push this build onto a real orchestration and observability stack: it is event-driven and real-time (a webhook, a two-minute SLA, 24/7), it is stateful and branching (agents hand off and take different paths per lead), and it is autonomous (no human checks each output on the standard path). LangGraph exists for exactly that: durable state, retries, checkpoints and human-in-the-loop interrupts on a live branching graph. And because no human reviews each reply, you need per-run tracing and quality alerts to catch a bad output after the fact. Examples 1 and 3 have none of those three properties, so they correctly use a scheduler plus lighter eval monitoring instead.

Two honest cautions live in this flow. Speed is not the goal, qualified speed is: a 90-second reply to a junk lead is worse than a five-minute reply to a real buyer. And human-out-of-the-loop is earned, not assumed; the autonomy bounds are enforced in code. A fully autonomous, prospect-facing agent is a posture some brands and regulated industries should not adopt, and the right answer for them is a human-on-the-loop variant where a rep approves the first touch. One data note: the Pocus signal score is richest for accounts already in your system, so for a genuinely cold new logo the agent leans more on live Apollo enrichment and its own reasoning than on a pre-computed score, and the qualification threshold should account for that.

The standard operating procedures that make it safe

An autonomous, prospect-facing system carries more risk than a supervised one, so its operating procedures are stricter. These are agreed before launch, not after.

  • Autonomy bounds, written down: the agent may enrich, qualify, reply and book within defined limits, and must escalate to a human on named exceptions (enterprise logo, existing open opportunity, pricing or legal questions, low confidence).
  • Quality gate over speed: a 90-second reply to a junk lead is worse than a 5-minute reply to a real buyer. The agent is measured on qualified speed-to-lead, not raw speed.
  • Fallback to human: if enrichment, scoring or confidence fails, the lead routes to a person with everything gathered so far, never into a silent void.
  • Guardrailed replies: the lead-facing agent answers from a governed knowledge source with prohibited-claims rules, and never negotiates price or terms.
  • Scoped identity and full audit: the agent acts as a non-human identity with least-privilege credentials, and every enrichment, message and booking is logged and attributable.
  • Kill switch and rate limits: defined triggers (hallucination, cost or latency spike, loop) alert a human, and one control halts the agent while leads fall back to manual routing.

GTME builds the multi-agent graph in LangGraph on a managed cloud service: triage, research, qualify and route agents that hand off, with per-lead state and a tiered model strategy (Haiku to classify and route, Sonnet to qualify and reply, Opus only for the hard calls). Business Systems stands up the SQS queue, the MCP gateway and Salesforce Hosted MCP access as a scoped integration user, and sets the autonomy bounds in enforceable code, not a document. RevOps defines qualification criteria, routing rules and the escalation triggers.

GTME is accountable for the agent graph. BizSys owns the queue, gateway and enforced guardrails. RevOps owns qualification and routing logic.
3

QA an autonomous, prospect-facing system

The bar is higher than a supervised build

Because there is no human on the standard path, QA is stricter. GTME red-teams the qualify and reply agents on a large golden set: does it escalate the enterprise logo, refuse the pricing question, never hallucinate a claim to a prospect. Business Systems runs the security and autonomy-bound review: can the agent only do what it is permitted. RevOps confirms the routing and escalation match reality. Nothing goes live until the escalation paths are proven.

GTME owns agent-behavior QA and red-teaming. BizSys owns security and autonomy QA. RevOps owns routing and escalation QA.
4

Launch on a slice, with a holdout and a human watching

RevOps owns rollout, all three watch closely

This launches narrow: one segment or region, with a holdout for clean measurement and a human monitoring live for the first weeks, ready to hit the kill switch. GTME watches LangSmith for latency, cost and quality drift in real time. Only once the qualified speed-to-lead and conversion hold does the scope widen. Autonomy is earned by evidence, not granted on day one.

RevOps owns the phased rollout and holdout. GTME watches live quality. BizSys stands by the kill switch.
5

Operate, monitor and widen

Shared ownership, tighter monitoring

An autonomous system needs closer running than a supervised one. GTME owns the evals, latency and cost dashboards and tunes the agents. Business Systems monitors platform health, rotates credentials and audits the autonomy bounds on a schedule. RevOps watches qualified speed-to-lead and inbound conversion, and decides when the agent has earned wider autonomy. That is the move from Phase 3 to Phase 4 for inbound.

GTME maintains the agents and their quality. BizSys maintains the platform and audits autonomy. RevOps owns the inbound outcome.

The standard operating procedures that make it safe

Because this writes to the CRM the whole company forecasts on, the procedures center on trust in the write-back and on keeping judgment with a human.

  • Human owns the commit: the agent preps, inspects and drafts, but a manager still calls the number. The forecast is a judgment, and judgment stays with a person.
  • Scoped, audited write-back: the agent writes only a named set of fields, never stage or amount without review, and every write is logged with the call evidence and a confidence score behind it.
  • Confidence gate: low-confidence extractions are flagged for a human rather than written silently, so the CRM never fills with confident-sounding errors.
  • Stage and exit criteria are fixed first: the agents are only as good as the definitions underneath them, so the forecast categories and stage exit criteria are agreed and frozen before any automation runs.
  • Evidence, not assertion: every flagged risk or field change links back to the specific call moment that justifies it, so a rep can contest it and a manager can trust it.
  • Reconciliation cadence: the agent's roll-up is checked against Gong Forecast and the human commit weekly, and drift between the three is investigated, not ignored.

The launch playbook: who leads, who supports

Same collaboration shape. The distinctive move here is freezing the definitions first, because no agent can compensate for a forecast category that shifts every quarter.

1

Shape the problem and freeze the definitions

RevOps leads, GTME and BizSys consult

Sales leadership is spending two afternoons a week on the forecast and still missing. RevOps names the real problem, which is almost always data quality rather than the model: stale fields, inconsistent stages, and no time to inspect. Critically, RevOps freezes the forecast categories and the stage exit criteria first, because no agent can compensate for definitions that shift every quarter. GTME confirms what Gong and Salesforce already do, so the build only adds what is missing. Business Systems scopes what the agent may write and what stays human.

RevOps owns the definitions and the accuracy goal. GTME maps buy-versus-build. BizSys scopes the write-back.
2

Build the agent layer on top of Gong and Salesforce

GTME builds, BizSys provisions, RevOps defines done

GTME builds three focused agents on top of the existing stack: extraction (fields from calls to Salesforce), inspection (evidence versus CRM), and commentary (the drafted narrative), using Haiku for bulk field work and Sonnet for reasoning. Business Systems provisions scoped write-back to a named field set, with least-privilege credentials and full audit logging. RevOps defines what good looks like: which fields, what a clean deal record contains, what must never be written without review.

GTME builds the agents. BizSys owns the scoped write-back and audit. RevOps owns the field definitions and definition of done.
3

All three sign before it touches the forecast

Because this writes to the CRM the whole company forecasts on, QA is strict. GTME validates extraction accuracy against a labeled set of calls and tunes the confidence threshold so low-confidence writes are flagged, not guessed. Business Systems confirms the agent can only write the named fields and that every write is audited. RevOps checks that the inspection flags and drafted commentary match how they actually read deals. Nothing writes to a live opportunity until the evidence links are solid.

GTME owns extraction and reasoning QA. BizSys owns write-back security QA. RevOps owns forecast-logic QA.
4

RevOps owns adoption, everyone watches the first cycles

This launches read-mostly: the agent drafts and flags, the manager approves every consequential change, and a holdout team runs the old manual scrub for a quarter so the accuracy and time-saved are proven, not assumed. GTME watches extraction quality and drift in the observability layer. Only once managers trust the brief does the write-back widen from suggestions to scoped automatic fields.

RevOps owns rollout and the holdout. GTME watches quality. BizSys stands by the audit and rollback.
5

Shared ownership, clear lanes

GTME tunes the agents and watches extraction accuracy as products and messaging change. Business Systems keeps the Gong and Salesforce connections healthy, rotates credentials and audits the write-back. RevOps runs the weekly reconciliation between the agent roll-up, Gong Forecast and the human commit, and owns the metric that justified it all: forecast accuracy, and the hours of prep given back to the team.

GTME maintains the agents. BizSys maintains the connections and audit. RevOps owns forecast accuracy.
Notice what stayed constant across all three builds: RevOps owned the problem and the outcome, Business Systems owned the platform and the guardrails, and GTM Engineering owned the build. The architecture changed completely each time; the collaboration did not. The job of a good GTM Engineer is to know which stack the work actually calls for, and to resist building more than it needs.
